# Use MPS and PPS agreement cautiously

**There is no defensible universal “typical” difference between MPS and Pauli-path simulation (PPS) OTOC estimates.** Measure the difference on matched observables in the two exact sample circuits, then inspect its distribution separately by circuit and precision setting. Small point-to-point MPS–PPS differences are useful as a screening signal, but they cannot certify closeness to the exact OTOC: two biased methods can agree, and PPS can appear converged across several thresholds before changing again. The practical stopping decision is whether to launch the *next* precision job after the current one finishes. I recommend requiring cross-method agreement, stability within **each** method across multiple precision levels, and stability of the whole circuit’s prediction vector. Calibrate those gates against the released official scorer on both sample circuits before enabling automatic stopping. ([BlueQubit-affiliated PPS study](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.10771v1); [BlueQubit SDK reference](https://app.bluequbit.io/sdk-docs/bluequbit.sdk.html))

## The two methods discard different information

Finite-bond MPS compresses the evolving **state** by retaining a limited number of Schmidt coefficients at each cut; its cost and error depend strongly on the entanglement generated by the circuit. PPS propagates the measured **operator** backward as a sum of Pauli terms and discards coefficients below a threshold; non-Clifford gates can proliferate terms. An OTOC can therefore be difficult for one representation and easier for the other. The gap varies with circuit, qubit, observable, and precision settings, so it is an empirical quantity rather than a transferable constant. ([Vidal](https://arxiv.org/abs/quant-ph/0301063); [BlueQubit SDK reference](https://app.bluequbit.io/sdk-docs/bluequbit.sdk.html); [BlueQubit-affiliated PPS study](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.10771v1))

For matched estimates, define the pointwise gap $d_q=|M_q-P_q|$, where $M_q$ and $P_q$ use the same official target key, observable definition, sign, and normalization. Report at least the median, a high quantile, and the maximum of $d_q$ per sample circuit and precision pair; also plot $d_q$ against each method’s *actual* error on the samples. This reveals whether small disagreement has historically meant small error **for this task**. Algebraically, $d_q$ only says that at least one method has error at least $d_q/2$; it gives no upper bound when the methods agree. If the exact value is 0.70, MPS returns 0.20, and PPS returns 0.21, the gap is 0.01 while both errors are near 0.5. Common mistakes in qubit indexing or OTOC construction can also make the methods agree on the same wrong quantity. BlueQubit-affiliated work specifically warns that PPS threshold sweeps can show **apparent**, nonmonotonic convergence. ([BlueQubit-affiliated PPS study](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.10771v1))

## Stop only after cross-method and within-method checks pass

At successive MPS bond dimensions $\chi_{k-2}<\chi_{k-1}<\chi_k$ and PPS thresholds $\epsilon_{j-2}>\epsilon_{j-1}>\epsilon_j$, compute the latest gap $d_q=|M_q(\chi_k)-P_q(\epsilon_j)|$. For each method, use the **larger of its last two step changes**: $u^M_q=\max(|M_q(\chi_k)-M_q(\chi_{k-1})|,|M_q(\chi_{k-1})-M_q(\chi_{k-2})|)$, and define $u^P_q$ analogously at fixed PPS transpilation level. Three levels per method make this a more meaningful screen than one small adjacent step. The proposed pointwise gate is

\[
\max_q d_q\le\tau_{\rm cross},\qquad
\max_q u^M_q\le\tau_M,\qquad
\max_q u^P_q\le\tau_P.
\]

These are **proposed empirical tolerances**, not mathematical error bounds. Start with absolute differences; ratios to $|M_q|$ or $|P_q|$ become unstable near zero. A predeclared scale floor derived from the samples can make tolerances dimensionless if circuit amplitudes differ substantially. Because PPS truncation can cross discrete coefficient thresholds, include an occasional deliberately finer **challenge run** after an apparent stop when credits permit; a failed challenge run revises the policy. ([BlueQubit-affiliated PPS study](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.10771v1))

Do not replace the maximum by a count such as “90% of qubits converged” without examining the remaining rows. One outlier can materially rotate a centered OTOC vector. Check $1-\rho(M_k,M_{k-1})$, $1-\rho(P_j,P_{j-1})$, and $1-\rho(M_k,P_j)$ as **shape diagnostics** when correlation matters to the official score; evaluate the official scorer itself on labeled samples. If a centered vector has a norm near the sample-derived numerical floor, flag it as nearly constant and use raw RMS and maximum changes instead: Pearson correlation is undefined for constant input and unstable for nearly constant input. Cross-method shape agreement can be high even if both shapes are wrong. ([SciPy Pearson reference](https://docs.scipy.org/doc/scipy/reference/generated/scipy.stats.pearsonr.html))

## Calibrate one rule on whole sample circuits

There are only **two labeled sample circuits** in the design, so qubits within a circuit must not be counted as independent validation circuits. Before tuning, choose a small tolerance grid and a permissible official-score loss $\eta$ that reflects the team’s compute-versus-score tradeoff. Replay the same MPS/PPS precision ladders that hidden circuits will use. Choose a stopping rule on sample A and test it unchanged on B, then reverse A and B. For each held-out circuit, compare the score at the first stage where the rule would stop with the best later affordable **direct-estimate** score; record the score loss, maximum qubit error, and compute saved. Enable automatic stopping only if the same conservative policy meets the predeclared loss margin on **both** held-out circuits and saves meaningful cost. Otherwise retain the highest trustworthy direct estimate and use the metrics only to prioritize jobs. Holding out entire circuits follows group-aware validation principles; repeatedly retuning to the two held-out scores would overfit them. ([scikit-learn cross-validation guide](https://scikit-learn.org/stable/modules/cross_validation.html))

No numeric $\tau_{\rm cross}$, $\tau_M$, $\tau_P$, shape tolerance, or claimed false-stop rate can be supplied before those exact sample outputs and the official `score_csv` are available. The pre-competition document correctly treats the scoring function and challenge wrapper as unconfirmed. The released kit and submission template should settle those facts; public BlueQubit SDK controls alone do not establish challenge-specific accuracy, batching, or billing. ([BlueQubit SDK reference](https://app.bluequbit.io/sdk-docs/bluequbit.sdk.html))

## Apply the rule at the platform’s billing unit

If the kit allows independent per-qubit jobs, freeze qubits that pass all gates and refine the unresolved ones. If a job computes the whole circuit or a bundle, the relevant choice is **one more whole job**: compare the remaining qubits’ likely effect on the *circuit score* with that job’s measured credits, queue time, and runtime. A high fraction of converged qubits does not create a saving if they are inseparable in execution. Keep a complete baseline CSV before refinement, and stop launching new jobs when the expected gain falls below other feasible actions or the submission buffer is reached. BlueQubit’s SDK documents asynchronous job polling, completed-job cost and timing fields, and cancellation requests, but I found no documented per-qubit streaming result, numerical accuracy certificate, or guarantee that canceling an in-flight job saves credits. Treat cancellation economics as unknown until the kit or a controlled test establishes them. ([BlueQubit SDK reference](https://app.bluequbit.io/sdk-docs/bluequbit.sdk.html))

## Conclusion

The best use of the MPS–PPS gap is to **rank and screen refinement decisions**, with an empirical stop gate backed by two independent precision histories and the full vector’s behavior. Passing that gate earns the label *stable at tested settings*; it does not establish accuracy on hidden circuits. The decisive experiment is a sample-circuit replay that measures how often the rule would have stopped before a later run improved the official score, and how much computation those stops would save.
